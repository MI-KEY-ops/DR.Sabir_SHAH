# **Workplace Professional Dilemma, Colleague Feedback and Reflection on Diabetes Mellitus** 

## **1. Introduction** 

During my clinical practice at Bajaur Clinic, I encountered a professional dilemma while managing a patient with diabetes mellitus who had poor glycaemic control and a diabetic foot ulcer. This case was challenging because the patient had limited knowledge about diabetes management, difficulties adhering to prescribed medication and challenges following dietary and lifestyle recommendations. 

This experience helped me understand that managing diabetes requires more than prescribing medication. It requires effective communication, patient education, empathy, ethical decision-making and continuous professional support. 

## **2. Description of the Professional Dilemma** 

In the course of my clinical practice, one patient with diabetes mellitus came with the complaint of poorly controlled blood glucose level and a diabetic foot ulcer. At the time of consultation, I noticed that the patient lacked an understanding of the importance of taking medications regularly, monitoring blood glucose, eating properly, staying active and proper foot care. Patients had problems to comply with the treatment plan and to practice self-care routinely. The financial and socioeconomic issues also seemed to have a negative impact on the patient's ability to control the disease. I had a dilemma in my professional life about whether I should spend most of my time prescribing treatment and telling the patient to follow medical directions or spend some more time getting to know the patient's personal and financial problems and come up with a realistic management and counselling plan. In the limited time and resources of the clinic, I felt that prescription of a drug was not sufficient to correct the patient's problems. Patient centred care was therefore emphasised in the form of listening to the patient's concerns, explaining how necessary it is to take their medication as directed, what they can eat on a budget, how important physical activity is, and education on how to look after their feet and any wounds that form. 

## **3. Professional and Ethical Issues** 

There were several professional principles at play in this circumstance: 

- Beneficence: Giving care that improves the health and wellbeing of a patient. 

- Non-maleficence: Avoiding additional complications, especially exacerbation of the DFU. 

- Autonomy: Respecting the patient's right to autonomous decision making regarding treatment and self care. 

- Justice: Taking into account the financial and social situation of the patient when making a recommendation for treatment. 

- Professional responsibility: Providing appropriate education, follow up and referral if necessary. 

- Confidentiality and dignity: Treating the patient with respect and without judgement, observing their privacy. 

That lesson has had a tremendous impact on me and has left me in awe that doctors and other health care workers should blame patients for not taking their medication when they have not been aware of the reasons. 

## **4. Feedback from a Colleague** 

Talked to another senior colleague about this case to get feedback on my management and communication strategy. As an example, colleagues may be asked to provide their suggestions for discussion and confirmation: My colleague valued my attempt to fully grasp the patient's challenges instead of pointing fingers at the patient for not taking the medication as prescribed. They noted that patient-centered care is essential for diabetes treatment, as each patient's health literacy, financial situation, lifestyle and capacity to adhere to care recommendations are unique. My colleague suggested that I do an improvement in patient counselling by using simple language and explaining the complications of uncontrolled diabetes and apply the teachback method to assess the patient's understanding. They also emphasised the need for frequent evaluation of DFUs, as well as keeping up patient's blood glucose monitoring and keeping clinical findings documented and arranging timely referral if complications occurred and specialist attention is needed. Additionally, my colleague suggested that family members be included with the consent of the patient, to assist with the process of taking medication, diet and daily foot care. 

## **5. Reflection on the Dilemma and Colleague's Feedback** 

Looking back on this experience, I thought to myself that one of the primary challenges faced by people with diabetes is the disconnect between good medicine and their ability to put it into practice in their day-to-day living. At first, I was treating the foot ulcer and my blood glucose level. But little by little I realized that the patient's knowledge, finances, lifestyle and emotional health were as significant. The professional quandary led me to wonder if I could adequately treat the patients' illnesses by just doing that. I found out that diabetes treatment is comprehensive, patient-centered and requires a holistic approach. Patients might be giving some attention to medical instructions but still be unable to carry them out due to financial constraints, not understanding the medical advice, family responsibilities, or their inability to 

obtain the necessary resources. It was helpful to get input from my fellow colleague, as it pointed out some areas to improve. I discovered that educating patients requires interaction and not instructions. The teach-back method can be used to check if patients have understood the medication plan, diet or foot care instructions. I also learned that it's crucial to include patients in creating attainable treatment goals. This was very similar to my previous learning with CHPE. In my diabetes reflection and qualitative research project “Exploring the Experiences and Barriers of Patients with Diabetes Mellitus in Glycemic Control and Diabetic Foot Care: A Qualitative Study”, I became familiar with the personal and social factors that impact diabetes self-management. It was eye-opening to learn the value of listening to the patients' experiences and understanding their perspective. The learning on communication and feedback that I had during the CHPE program also had an impact on how I approached it. I knew that colleagues' positive comments could highlight areas for development of clinical work and professional practice. Likewise, my education on assessment has taught me that knowledge, understanding, and awareness of the patient needs to be assessed and not assumed. The CPD framework also emphasised the need for reflection, planning, learning and assessment as key components of the continuous professional development process. I now know that to better care for patients with diabetes, it is necessary to update medical knowledge; to build communication skills; and to assess the impact of patient education. Overall, this case helped me to build my confidence in chronic disease management and my awareness of my professional responsibilities, empathy and patient centred care. 

## **6. Action Plan for Future Practice** 

To improve my future practice, I will: 

1. Deliver individualized diabetes education based on their knowledge, income and lifestyle of each patient. 

2. Assess patients' comprehension of the medication, diet and foot care using the teachback method. 

3. Promote frequent blood sugar testing and adherence to medications. 

4. Educate patients on the importance of checking their feet daily, good foot hygiene and early detection of wounds/infection. 

5. Thoroughly evaluate diabetic foot problems and make timely referral if necessary. 

6. Educate family members about Diabetes with permission of the patient. 

7. Regularly consult senior colleagues and use their advice to practice. 

8. Attend continuing professional development events focused on diabetes management and diabetes patient education. 

9. Perform periodic follow-up and review if the patient is meeting realistic self management objectives. 

## **7. Conclusion** 

It was a good learning experience for me in my CHPE journey that I am facing this professional dilemma. It was a class that made me realize that the control of diabetes does not only involve taking pills or monitoring blood sugar. It also involves an understanding of what patients experience in practice, the ability to create solutions to challenges with self-care and on-going education and support. The feedback from my colleague helped to bring into my awareness the value of good communication, individual counselling, team working and the timely management of diabetic complications. My dream of becoming a medical specialist is to become a competent doctor who will be able to offer ethical, evidence based and patient centred treatment and will be able to contribute to the betterment of diabetes education and healthcare in Bajaur in the future.

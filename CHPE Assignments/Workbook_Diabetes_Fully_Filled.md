# **Page 3 – Phase-I: Definitions** 

## **Research** 

A systematic process of identifying a problem, collecting and analysing information, and drawing conclusions to increase knowledge and improve practice. 

## **Qualitative Research** 

A research approach used to explore people’s experiences, perceptions, beliefs, feelings and behaviours using non-numerical data. 

## **Quantitative Research** 

A research approach that measures variables numerically and uses statistical methods to describe, compare or test relationships. 

## **Observation** 

A planned and systematic process of watching and recording behaviours, interactions, conditions or events in a clinical or natural setting. 

## **Theory** 

An organised set of concepts and propositions that helps explain a phenomenon or relationships between concepts. 

# **Page 4 – Definitions continued** 

## **Hypothesis** 

A tentative, testable statement about an expected relationship between variables. A formal hypothesis is usually not required in qualitative research. 

## **Methodology** 

The overall rationale and strategy for conducting research, including research design, sampling, data collection and analysis. 

## **Ontology** 

A view about the nature of reality and what can be considered real or existing. 

## **Epistemology** 

A view about the nature, sources and limits of knowledge and how knowledge can be obtained. 

## **Positivism** 

A philosophical position that emphasises objective reality, measurement, observation and empirical testing. 

# **Page 5 – Definitions continued** 

## **Post-Positivism** 

A position recognising that reality may exist independently, but knowledge is partial and influenced by context, measurement and limitations. 

## **Constructivism** 

A perspective that views knowledge and meaning as constructed through people’s experiences, interactions and social contexts. 

## **Explorative Studies** 

Studies designed to explore an issue, experience or problem in depth when it is not sufficiently understood. 

## **Difference in research design** 

Qualitative research explores experiences, meanings and context with flexible methods. Quantitative research uses predefined variables, structured measurements and statistical analysis. 

# **Page 6 – Research design comparison** 

## **Qualitative approach** 

Uses open-ended questions, interviews, observations and thematic interpretation. It asks questions such as: What do patients experience? What barriers do they describe? 

## **Quantitative approach** 

Uses numerical measurements, structured questionnaires and statistical analysis. It asks questions such as: How common is poor glycemic control? Is one variable associated with another? 

## **Application to diabetes** 

The proposed study needs qualitative methods because it seeks to understand patients’ experiences and perceived barriers to glycemic control and diabetic foot care. 

# **Page 7 – Role of hypothesis** 

## **Role of a hypothesis in qualitative research** 

A formal hypothesis is generally not central to qualitative research. The researcher starts with an open question and may use perspectives or sensitising concepts. In this study, possible influences such as diabetes knowledge, medication use, lifestyle, foot-care knowledge and emotional concerns will be explored rather than assumed to be proven causes. 

# **Page 8 – Qualitative research designs** 

## **Phenomenology** 

Explores the lived experience of a phenomenon. 

## **Grounded theory** 

Develops an explanatory theory from systematically collected and analysed qualitative data. 

## **Ethnography** 

Explores behaviours, meanings and practices within a cultural or social group. 

## **Case study** 

Provides an in-depth investigation of a bounded case, setting or group. 

## **Qualitative descriptive design** 

Provides a straightforward description of participants’ experiences and perspectives. This is the most suitable design for the present study. 

# **Page 9 – Qualitative vs quantitative designs** 

## **Qualitative** 

Flexible design; small purposive samples; interviews/observations; data expressed as words; coding and themes; emphasis on meaning and context. 

## **Quantitative** 

Structured design; sample size often based on statistical requirements; numerical measurements; predefined variables; statistical analysis; emphasis on measurement and relationships. 

## **Diabetes study** 

A qualitative descriptive design will allow patients to explain their daily experiences with glycemic control and foot care in their own words. 

# **Page 10 – Quantitative data collection methods** 

## **Methods** 

Structured questionnaires, surveys, clinical measurements, blood glucose/HbA1c measurements, laboratory investigations, structured observations, medical-record review, standardised assessments and experiments. 

## **Possible diabetes example** 

A quantitative diabetes study could measure HbA1c and administer a validated self-management questionnaire. 

**Page 11 – Qualitative data collection methods** 

## **Methods** 

Semi-structured individual interviews, in-depth interviews, focus-group discussions, observation, field notes and document or narrative review. 

## **Selected method** 

Semi-structured individual interviews will be used because they allow patients to describe personal experiences, barriers and support needs in depth. 

# **Page 12 – Sample size** 

## **Quantitative research** 

Sample size is commonly determined using statistical considerations such as power analysis. 

## **Qualitative research** 

Sample size is usually smaller and is guided by information richness and saturation. 

## **Proposed diabetes sampling** 

Approximately 10–20 participants may be anticipated initially, but the final number will depend on thematic saturation and the richness of the interviews. 

## **Important point** 

The number should not be treated as a fixed statistical requirement; recruitment will stop when additional interviews no longer provide important new information. 

# **Page 13 – Data management** 

## **Qualitative data management** 

Manage audio recordings, transcripts, field notes and coding documents. Use participant codes rather than names, keep files securely stored and remove identifying information. 

## **Quantitative data management** 

Usually involves structured numerical datasets with predefined variables, data-entry procedures and quality checks. 

## **Diabetes study** 

Interview recordings will be transcribed, checked, de-identified and securely stored. Each participant will receive a code such as P01, P02 and P03. 

# **Page 14 – Data analysis** 

## **Qualitative analysis** 

Transcribe interviews; read repeatedly; identify meaningful statements; assign codes; group related codes into categories; develop and review themes; define themes; and report findings with anonymised quotations. 

## **Quantitative analysis** 

Uses descriptive and inferential statistical techniques to summarise and test numerical data. 

## **Selected diabetes analysis** 

Thematic analysis will be used to identify patterns concerning glycemic control, medication, diet, exercise, glucose monitoring, foot care, emotional experiences and healthcare support. 

# **Page 15 – Quality of research** 

## **Credibility** 

Findings should accurately represent participants’ experiences. Use careful interviewing, member clarification where appropriate and repeated engagement with the data. 

## **Dependability** 

Keep a clear record of research decisions, interview procedures, coding and analysis. 

## **Confirmability** 

Show that interpretations are grounded in participants’ data rather than researcher preference. 

## **Transferability** 

Provide enough description of the participants and setting for readers to judge whether findings may apply elsewhere. 

## **Other safeguards** 

Reflexivity, transparent methods, appropriate sampling and ethical conduct. 

# **Page 16 – Linguistic turn** 

## **Meaning** 

The linguistic turn recognises the central role of language in how people describe, interpret and communicate knowledge and reality. 

## **Application** 

Patients’ words are important qualitative data because they communicate their understanding, beliefs, worries and experiences of diabetes. 

## **Diabetes example** 

The way patients describe diet, medication, foot care, symptoms and complications may reveal meanings that cannot be captured fully by numerical measurements. 

**Page 17 – Qualitative and quantitative are not opposites** 

## **Answer** 

Both approaches are systematic ways of answering research questions. Quantitative research can show the extent or frequency of a problem, while qualitative research can explain experiences and meanings behind those patterns. 

## **Diabetes example** 

A quantitative study may show poor glycemic control through HbA1c results. A qualitative study can explore why patients find glucose control difficult and what support they need.

# **Page 28 – Definitions, research approach and sampling** 

## **Key definitions** 

Diabetes Mellitus: a chronic metabolic condition requiring ongoing management. Glycemic control: management of blood glucose toward appropriate targets. Diabetic foot care: preventive practices intended to maintain foot health and reduce complications. Self-management: daily actions undertaken to manage a chronic condition. Barrier: a factor that makes self-management more difficult. 

## **Research design** 

Qualitative descriptive study. 

## **Population** 

Adults diagnosed with Diabetes Mellitus receiving care in the selected clinical setting. 

## **Sampling plan** 

Purposive sampling. 

## **Inclusion criteria** 

Adults with Diabetes Mellitus who can communicate their experiences and provide informed consent. 

## **Exclusion criteria** 

Patients unable to provide informed consent or participate meaningfully, or patients who are clinically unstable and for whom participation would cause undue burden. 

## **Saturation** 

Recruitment will continue until additional interviews no longer produce important new themes. 

# **Page 29 – Variables, instrumentation, analysis and limitations** 

## **Variables/concepts** 

In qualitative research, fixed independent and dependent variables are not the main focus. Concepts to explore include diabetes knowledge, medication adherence, diet, exercise, glucose monitoring, foot care, emotional experiences, family support, healthcare support and access to care. 

## **Instrumentation** 

Semi-structured interview guide, participant information sheet, consent form, demographic form, audio recorder when consented, and field-note guide. 

## **Example interview questions** 

Tell me about your experience of living with diabetes. What makes it difficult or easy to control your blood glucose? How do you look after your feet? What support do you receive? What would help you manage diabetes better? 

## **Analysis** 

Transcribe, familiarise, code, categorise and develop themes using thematic analysis. 

## **Limitations** 

Small purposive sample, possible socially desirable responses, recall or communication difficulties, contextual specificity and researcher interpretation may limit transferability. 

# **Page 30 – Ethics, action plan and finances** 

## **Ethics** 

Obtain ethical approval; obtain informed consent; ensure voluntary participation and the right to withdraw; protect confidentiality; securely store research data; minimise emotional or physical burden; and declare conflicts of interest.

Potential costs include printing, transport, communication, stationery, recording/storage and transcription. Existing institutional resources should be used where possible. Any external funding should be declared.

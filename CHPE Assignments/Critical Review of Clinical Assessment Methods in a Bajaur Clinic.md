# **Critical Review of Clinical Assessment Methods in a Bajaur Clinic** 

## **Introduction** 

Safe and effective patient care is essential and a key element of clinical assessment. Patients come to me at my Bajaur clinic for a variety of acute and chronic ailments such as diabetes, (COPD), Parkinson's disease, anaemia during pregnancy, prostate related complaints, and complaints related to gastrointestinal issues. These experiences have emphasized the need to have a systematic, patient-centred, evidence-informed approach to assessment. This critical review will explore the assessment process that is undertaken in my clinical environment based on the key principles of the assessment process, which are History Taking, Physical Examination, Appropriate Investigations, Clinical Reasoning, Communication, Risk Assessment, Documentation and Follow-up. 

## **History-Taking** 

In my own practice I use history taking as one of the most common assessment techniques. My first task is to determine the primary symptom presented by the patient and then investigate the onset, duration, intensity, progression, symptoms, past illness, medications, allergies and family and social history. My reflective cases illustrated the value of the history taking to the presenting problem. These included gastrointestinal causes like dyspepsia or peptic ulcer disease in an older patient with epigastric pain radiating to the back, and the need to think of the other possibilities including pancreatitis, biliary disease and cardiac. Similarly, the assessment of a COPD patient should include data on breathlessness, cough, sputum, smoking exposure, previous exacerbations, hospitalisation and medication use. The advantage of taking a history is that it is low cost, readily obtainable, and doesn't require high-tech equipment. But it relies on the ability of the clinician to communicate and the patient to be able to describe the symptoms accurately. The quality of the information gained can also be limited by time constraints, language barriers, health literacy and cultural factors. So I will need to start off with open questions, ask focused questions, keep things private, and respect patients' concerns. 

## **Physical Examination** 

Objective data (physical exam) adds to the patient's history. I do spot checks on vital signs and do focused exams on the presenting complaint in my clinic routine. These can include respiratory examination in COPD, abdominal examination for gastrointestinal symptoms, CVD examination when appropriate, and general examination in the patient with chronic diseases. Key limitations of investigations is that they can't find clinically important findings. For instance, signs of deterioration, such as respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, chest examination and general appearance, will help detect deterioration in a patient with COPD. But physical examination is not always helpful. The findings might be subjective, depending on the clinician's experience. Some conditions may have few physical signs in their early stages. Examination alone should not be used, but rather interpreted with the history and where relevant, investigations. 

## **Use of Investigations** 

Investigations are important to do when clinical information is not enough. Investigations in my setting will vary depending on presentation and availability and may include blood glucose and HbA1c for diabetes, complete blood count for suspected anaemia, renal and liver function tests, ECG for appropriate chest or upper abdominal symptoms and imaging or other investigations as clinically indicated. In some cases, symptoms cannot be used as a reliable diagnostic sign of an infection, such as suspected H. pylori infection. Appropriate testing may be required. Similarly, an elderly patient who complains of epigastric pain radiating to the back should be evaluated for potentially serious alternative diagnoses before making a diagnosis of gastrointestinal disease. The largest drawback to my Bajaur clinic is the fact that certain lab tests, imaging and specialist services may be limited or need a referral. This may subject the patient to a delay in their definitive diagnosis. Therefore, uncertainty and appropriate referral should be considered as part of clinical assessment. 

## **Clinical Reasoning and Risk Assessment** 

One of the key principles of assessment is the ability to recognise common conditions from potentially serious illness. From the reflective cases I have experienced it is clear that the diagnosis should not be made just because of the most apparent symptom. For instance, COPD symptoms might be a normal chronic disease, but can also be a sign of an exacerbation or other severe respiratory diseases. Likewise, pain in the upper stomach area (epigastric) may be a symptom of dyspepsia, but at times it could be a sign of more serious gastrointestinal, pancreatic, biliary or cardiac disease. Therefore, I look at red flag symptoms, age, comorbidities, vital signs, severity and progression of symptoms when determining whether a patient can be managed in the clinic or if further investigation and/or referral is needed. This risk stratification approach promotes patient safety and decreases early closure for diagnosis. 

## **Patient-Centred Assessment** 

Assessment should engage the patient, not be a clinical data-gathering process. I attempt to present the causes of symptoms, investigations, treatment and follow-up in a comprehensive and comprehensible way in my practice. This is especially true for chronic conditions like diabetes and COPD, where patient education and adherence, lifestyle changes, and ongoing monitoring play a significant role in their management. Treatment plans can be more realistic if patient preferences are respected and social and financial aspects taken into account. It can be difficult, however, to undertake a patient-centred assessment during a busy consultation 

and/or if the patient has limited health literacy. I am therefore going to seek to enhance my use of simple explanations, active listening and confirmation of understanding. 

## **Documentation and Follow-Up** 

Documentation is another key concept of assessment. Continuity of care and clinical accountability is supported by documenting the presenting complaint, investigations, examination findings, clinical impression, safety-netting advice, management plan and follow-up, as these help to provide continuity and clinical accountability. For chronic conditions and symptoms that haven't been resolved, follow-up is especially important. For instance, the person who is diabetic or COPD should be assessed continuously, and not just through a single consultation. Likewise, if gastrointestinal symptoms are persistent, patients should be reevaluated if symptoms don't improve or if some alarm features appear. 

## **Critical Reflection and Areas for Improvement** 

I feel that the best way to approach the assessment of a case is using history taking, focused physical examination, and timely investigations. There's room for improvement. I need to enhance the consistency and effectiveness of my documentation, expand systematic recording of red-flag symptoms, and make more use of validated assessment measures as appropriate. I must be open to the possibility of having no answers, especially in the absence of investigations. I should not make an assumption, but explicitly differentiate between a working diagnosis and a confirmed diagnosis. If there is any doubt about the diagnosis or if the patient is showing a very high risk of developing serious disease then referral should be considered early. 

## **Conclusion** 

Flexibility with systematic approach is needed in clinical evaluation in my Bajaur clinic. Safe patient care involves history taking, physical examination, investigations, clinical reasoning, risk assessment, communication, documentation and follow-up. In my experience with patients who have diabetes, COPD, Parkinsons disease, anaemia, prostate-related symptoms and epigastric pain, assessment needs to be tailored to the individual based on their symptoms, risk factors and clinical situation and their age. Future plans are to further build a structured approach to assessment, enhance documentation and safety netting, engage the patient in choices, and be aware of the need for further resources in my setting. Most importantly, I will strive to make an early diagnosis of serious illness without hasty diagnosis and to provide follow up or referral when indicated.
